A tiny language model learns one thing over and over: given the text it has seen so far, guess the next character. It is never told a rule of grammar and it is never given a dictionary. Instead it is shown millions of small examples of the form "given this prefix, here is what actually came next," and it slowly adjusts its internal numbers so that its guesses get closer to the truth.

At the start, the model knows nothing. Its weights are random numbers, so its first guesses are random too. If you ask it to continue the word "hello" it might output "hello xqzw" or any other nonsense. Training is the process of nudging those random numbers, one small step at a time, so that the guesses stop being random and start matching the patterns that are actually present in the training text.

Why does predicting the next character teach a model so much? Because language is full of structure. Some letters follow other letters far more often than chance would suggest. Some words follow other words. Some sentences follow a shape that fits the sentences around them. A model that gets very good at predicting the next character has, almost as a side effect, absorbed a huge amount of that structure. It has learned that "q" is almost always followed by "u". It has learned that a capital letter usually starts a new sentence. Later, with enough scale, it learns much more subtle patterns too.

This project is intentionally starting small. The model we are building has a tiny vocabulary, a short context window, and only a few layers. It will not write essays or hold a conversation. What it can do, if the pipeline is correct, is slowly lower its training loss on this very corpus, and eventually produce short stretches of text that look a little more like this corpus than random noise does. That is the entire goal of this phase: prove that raw text can be cleaned, turned into tokens, split into training and validation sets, chopped into fixed-length sequences, and handed to a model in batches, ready for the training loop that comes later.

Every sequence the model will see during training is built the same way. Take a window of context_length tokens as the input. Take the same window, shifted one position to the right, as the target. At every single position in that window, the model is being asked the same tiny question: given everything up to here, what comes next? The loss function, which we will implement in a later phase, simply measures how wrong each of those guesses was, averaged across the whole batch.

Splitting the data into a training set and a validation set matters because a model can memorize its training data without actually learning anything general. If we only ever check the loss on the same text the model was trained on, we would not be able to tell the difference between real learning and simple memorization. The validation set is held out, never shown to the model during training, so that the validation loss gives an honest signal about whether the model is generalizing at all, even at this tiny scale.

None of this requires a large corpus to demonstrate. A small amount of original text is enough to build and test every stage of the pipeline: reading raw text from disk, cleaning it into a consistent form, building a character vocabulary from whatever characters actually appear, encoding the cleaned text into integer token ids, splitting those ids into train and validation portions, and finally slicing the id sequences into fixed-length windows that a PyTorch Dataset and DataLoader can serve in batches. Once that pipeline is verified end to end on this small corpus, the exact same code will work on a larger corpus later, without changes, because nothing about the pipeline depends on the specific content of the text, only on its length and its character set.

That earlier corpus was deliberately tiny, just enough text to prove the pipeline worked. What follows is additional material, written for a specific purpose: to test a hypothesis. After training this project's model for three hundred steps on that small corpus, the training loss kept falling while the validation loss flattened out early and stayed flat. That gap between the two curves is the signature of overfitting: a model with far more parameters than the corpus has unique characters to teach it will, sooner or later, start memorizing the specific sequences it has seen rather than learning something that generalizes to sequences it has not seen. The obvious question is whether simply giving the model more text to learn from, while changing nothing else about its size or its training schedule, narrows that gap. This experiment exists to answer that question honestly, with a real measurement rather than a guess.

Attention, the mechanism at the center of this whole architecture, deserves a second look now that the basics are familiar. Each position in a sequence produces three vectors from its own embedding: a query, a key, and a value, each the result of a separate learned linear projection. The query is best thought of as a question the position is asking of the rest of the sequence. The key is the answer each position offers about what information it holds. When a query and a key point in a similar direction in their shared vector space, their dot product is large, and that position's value vector gets pulled strongly into the output. When they point in unrelated directions, the dot product is small, and that value contributes almost nothing. Multi-head attention simply runs several of these query-key-value systems side by side, each in its own smaller slice of the embedding dimension, so that different heads are free to specialize. One head might end up tracking the token immediately before the current one, a purely local pattern. Another might learn to look much further back for a repeated word or phrase. Because the heads are computed independently and only combined at the very end, nothing forces them to specialize in this way, but in practice, across enough data and enough training, useful specializations tend to emerge on their own, discovered by gradient descent rather than designed by hand.

Tokenization is a choice with consequences that ripple through the rest of the system. This project uses a character-level tokenizer, which keeps the vocabulary tiny and nearly eliminates the unknown-token problem, since almost any text made of the same alphabet can be encoded without loss. The price is sequence length: the same sentence takes roughly four to five times as many tokens to represent at the character level as it would at the word or subword level, which means the model's fixed context window covers proportionally less actual content. A subword tokenizer, built with an algorithm like byte pair encoding, sits between these two extremes. It starts from individual characters and repeatedly merges the most frequent adjacent pair into a new token, building up a vocabulary of a few thousand to a few tens of thousands of entries that captures common words and word fragments directly. Common words become single tokens; rare or unfamiliar words fall back to being spelled out from smaller pieces, so there is still no true unknown-token problem, just a gradual increase in tokens spent on unfamiliar material. This project will build and compare a subword tokenizer in a later phase, once there is a large enough and representative enough corpus that the comparison actually means something. Right now, character-level remains the right choice: simple, fully covering, and easy to reason about while the rest of the pipeline is still being proven.

Scaling behavior in language models has been studied carefully enough that some rough rules of thumb have emerged, and they are worth knowing even at this tiny scale, if only to understand why the choices made in this project look the way they do. Holding architecture roughly fixed, a model's loss tends to fall smoothly as either its parameter count or the amount of training data it sees increases, following something close to a power law over a wide range. Crucially, these two levers are not interchangeable: a model with a great many parameters trained on very little data will overfit, exactly as this project's own three-hundred-step run demonstrated, while a small model trained on an enormous amount of data will eventually run out of capacity to keep improving, plateauing well before the data has taught it everything it could. Somewhere between those two failure modes is a roughly appropriate ratio of parameters to training tokens for a given compute budget. Large-scale research has produced concrete estimates of what that ratio looks like at the scale of billions of parameters and hundreds of billions of tokens, and while this project's numbers are many orders of magnitude smaller, the same underlying tension applies here, at a scale small enough to observe directly in a single afternoon's experiment instead of a data center's worth of compute.

Consider what the training loop is actually doing, step by step, once the abstractions are stripped away. A batch of token sequences arrives, each one a fixed-length window paired with its shifted target. The model performs a forward pass, embedding each token, adding positional information, passing the result through however many transformer blocks are configured, and producing a probability distribution over the vocabulary at every position. The loss function compares that distribution against the one true next token at each position and produces a single number summarizing how wrong the model was, averaged across every position in the batch. Backpropagation then computes, for every one of the model's parameters, the partial derivative of that loss with respect to that parameter: a precise answer to the question of which direction, and how strongly, nudging this particular number would make the loss go down. The optimizer takes all of those gradients and actually performs the nudge, one parameter at a time, scaled by the learning rate and, in the case of Adam-style optimizers, further adjusted by a running estimate of how noisy or how consistent that parameter's gradient has been recently. Repeat this a few hundred times and the model's weights have moved, in tiny increments, from a random starting point toward a point in that vast parameter space where the training loss is measurably lower. Nothing about this process involves the model understanding anything in a human sense. It is closer to a very high-dimensional, gradual search, guided at every step by the single signal of how wrong the last guess was.

Evaluation exists to answer a question the training loss cannot answer on its own: has any of this generalized? A model can drive its training loss arbitrarily close to zero simply by memorizing its training set, given enough parameters and enough steps, without learning anything that would help it on text it has never seen. The validation set, held out from training entirely, is the only honest check against that failure mode. Perplexity restates the validation loss in a more intuitive unit: it is the exponential of the loss, and it can be read as the effective number of equally likely choices the model is, on average, choosing between at each position. A model with a validation perplexity of twenty is, loosely speaking, about as uncertain as if it were picking uniformly at random from twenty options at every step, even though the real vocabulary might be much larger or smaller than twenty; a model with a validation perplexity of two is far more confident and, if that confidence is earned rather than lucky, considerably more useful. Comparing perplexity across two checkpoints trained under different conditions, such as different amounts of training data, is one of the cleanest ways to see whether a change actually helped, expressed as a single comparable number rather than a raw loss value whose scale depends on the exact vocabulary size in use.

Sampling strategy shapes what generated text looks like just as much as the model's underlying quality does, and it is worth remembering that the two are genuinely separate concerns. Greedy decoding, which always takes the single most likely next token, is deterministic and can be useful for debugging, but it is also prone to a specific, easily observed failure: once the model finds itself in a state where the most likely continuation of a common short word is that same word again, greedy decoding will repeat it forever, because nothing in the decoding process ever considers a second-best option. Temperature sampling introduces controlled randomness by rescaling the logits before the softmax, and it trades a small amount of the model's raw confidence for a considerable improvement in variety and, often, felt naturalness. Top-k and top-p sampling both aim to keep that randomness from wandering into obviously implausible territory by restricting the candidate pool before sampling, one with a fixed count and the other with a probability-mass threshold that adapts to how confident the model actually is at each step. None of these strategies can make a weak model's output good; they can only decide how a given model's genuine uncertainty gets turned into an actual sequence of characters.

The hardware this project runs on is a real constraint, not an abstract one, and it has shaped almost every design decision made so far. Roughly eight gigabytes of total system memory, frequently with less than one gigabyte free at any given moment, rules out anything resembling the model sizes used in modern research or production systems without a second thought. A small graphics card with only two gigabytes of dedicated memory, paired with a driver old enough to make modern CUDA toolkits a real compatibility question, made the choice to train entirely on the CPU, at least for now, the more defensible option: predictable, debuggable, and free of an entire category of driver-related failure modes that would have been difficult to diagnose remotely. None of this is a permanent ceiling. It simply means every choice made in this project, including the size of this very corpus, has to earn its keep against a visible, honestly reported resource budget rather than being chosen for its own sake.

There is a broader point buried inside this specific experiment that is worth stating plainly. It would have been easy to treat the earlier overfitting result as a simple problem to patch, perhaps by adding stronger regularization, or by shrinking the model until it stopped memorizing its tiny corpus. Both of those would have worked, in the narrow sense of closing the gap between training and validation loss. Neither would have taught the same lesson this experiment is designed to teach: that the amount and quality of training data is not a minor implementation detail sitting alongside architecture and optimizer choices, but very often the single most consequential lever available, especially at small scale, where a model's capacity to memorize can so easily outrun a small corpus's capacity to teach it anything general. Measuring that directly, with the exact same architecture, the exact same training schedule, and nothing changed except how much text the model gets to read, is what makes this an experiment rather than a guess.

Checkpointing deserves its own explanation, separate from training itself, because it answers a question that only becomes urgent once a run takes long enough to worry about. A checkpoint is not simply a saved copy of the model's weights. It has to be self-describing enough that training can resume from it and continue exactly as if it had never stopped, which means it needs the optimizer's internal state as well as the model's, not just the model's. An optimizer like the one used here keeps a running average of each parameter's recent gradients and a running average of the squared magnitude of those gradients, and it uses both to decide how large a step to take for every individual parameter at every step. If only the model weights were saved and reloaded, training would technically resume, but the optimizer would start those running averages over from nothing, effectively forgetting weeks or months of accumulated context about how each parameter has been behaving, and the loss would visibly jump upward for a while as the optimizer relearned that context from scratch. Saving both pieces of state together, along with the exact step and epoch counters and the configuration that produced them, is what makes a checkpoint a true snapshot rather than a partial one.

This project's own history is a useful case study in why that distinction matters and why it is worth testing carefully rather than assuming it works. An earlier phase built the training loop and its checkpointing logic, wrote a reasonably thorough set of automated tests, and had every one of those tests pass. A subsequent, more adversarial review of the same code, looking specifically for scenarios the original tests had not tried, found several real problems anyway. One was subtle: resuming training with a deliberately different learning rate silently reverted to whatever learning rate had been saved in the checkpoint, because the optimizer's own state-loading logic overwrites every one of its hyperparameters, not just its running averages, and nothing in the original code corrected for that afterward. Another was a bookkeeping error: resuming after a training run that had already finished all of its planned epochs naturally would replay the final epoch a second time, because the code that decided which epoch to resume from could not tell the difference between an epoch that had been cut short partway through and one that had finished cleanly. Neither bug was visible from the outside during ordinary use, and neither would have been caught by tests that only ever exercised the easy, common case. The lesson is not that the original tests were worthless; every one of them was checking something real and correct. The lesson is that passing tests only prove the absence of the specific failures those tests were written to catch, and that a second, more skeptical pass, deliberately looking for scenarios the first pass did not imagine, is often what turns a plausible implementation into a trustworthy one.

Gradient accumulation is worth revisiting too, because it is one of the more mechanically fiddly parts of a training loop and because this project's own attempt at it needed correcting for exactly that reason. The idea is straightforward in principle: if the desired batch size does not fit comfortably in available memory, split it into several smaller micro-batches, run each one forward and backward without resetting the accumulated gradient in between, and only take an actual optimizer step once every micro-batch in that window has contributed. Done correctly, the resulting parameter update should be indistinguishable from what a single pass over the full combined batch would have produced. The subtlety is in exactly how each micro-batch's contribution gets weighted before it is added in. Dividing every micro-batch's loss by a fixed count, on the assumption that every micro-batch is the same size, quietly breaks down the moment one micro-batch in a window is smaller than the others, which happens naturally whenever a dataset's size is not an exact multiple of the batch size and the last batch of an epoch is left ragged. The correct approach weights each micro-batch by how many actual token predictions it contributed, not by a fixed assumed count, and only normalizes by the true total at the very end of the window. It is a small piece of arithmetic, easy to get wrong in a way that still runs without crashing and still produces a falling loss curve, and precisely for that reason it deserves the kind of direct, numeric verification that compares the accumulated result against a single equivalent large batch computed independently, rather than trusting that a plausible-looking implementation is automatically a correct one.

It is worth being honest about what a model of this size, trained on a corpus of this size, can and cannot be expected to do, even after this experiment's larger corpus is in place. Coherent, grammatically reliable, semantically meaningful generation is a capability that has, historically, required both very large models and very large amounts of training data, measured in the billions of parameters and the hundreds of billions of tokens for the systems most people are now familiar with. Nothing about a model with under a million parameters, however carefully engineered, is going to cross that gap, no matter how much this particular corpus grows within the bounds of what can reasonably be authored by hand and processed on this hardware in a single sitting. What this experiment can reasonably hope to show is a narrower, still genuinely informative claim: that the specific overfitting pattern observed earlier, the widening gap between falling training loss and flat validation loss, narrows when the model has more distinct material to learn from, everything else held constant. That is a real, measurable, mechanistic finding about this system, not a claim about approaching anything resembling fluent language generation, and the two should never be confused with each other, here or in any report about a small model's behavior.
